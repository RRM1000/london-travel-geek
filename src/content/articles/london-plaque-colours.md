Most people assume the blue ones are official and everything else is not. It is a reasonable assumption and it is wrong in both directions: **most of London's blue plaques are not English Heritage plaques**, and some genuine English Heritage plaques are brown, black or stone.

Colour is a much weaker signal than it looks. What it usually tells you is **who paid for the plaque**, and that is worth knowing — it is the difference between a national scheme with a twenty-year waiting rule and a local society commemorating a much-loved pub.

> 💡 **The Short Version:** Of the **3,630** plaques on our map of London, **2,045 are blue** — but only **905** of those come from the official scheme and its predecessors, so **most blue plaques are not official ones**. **Green** is mostly Westminster. **Brown** is mostly Hackney, plus the surviving Victorian originals. Everything else is boroughs, trusts and societies, and a **film cell** shape means the British Film Institute.

## Blue does not mean official

Blue is the default assumption because the official scheme made it famous. But the official scheme does not own the colour, and plenty of other bodies use it:

* **The City of London Corporation** — **163** plaques in the Square Mile, **158 of them blue**. The City is excluded from the English Heritage scheme entirely and runs its own, which is why Dr Johnson's is the only official plaque inside the City boundary.
* **The Nubian Jak Community Trust** — **38** in London, **34 of them blue**. Founded to commemorate Black and minority-ethnic history, starting with Bob Marley at County Hall in 2006.
* **The Heritage Foundation** — **41** in London, **36 blue**. Entertainers, comedians and TV names, many of whom the official scheme would not consider.
* **Borough councils** — Camden's are blue and are routinely mistaken for official ones.

All of these are legitimate. None of them are English Heritage.

> ⚠️ **The giveaway is the wording, not the colour.** Official plaques carry the English Heritage name on the plaque itself. If it does not say so, it is somebody else's — which does not make it wrong, just different.

## What the official scheme actually looks like

The official plaques in our dataset — the English Heritage scheme, plus the London County Council and Greater London Council that ran it before — come to **989 in London**. The overwhelming majority, **905**, are blue. But **84 are not**: the same scheme's older plaques include **37 brown, 15 black, 9 stone, 7 grey, 7 bronze, 5 white, 3 green and 1 brass**.

That is not inconsistency, it is chronology. **Blue ceramic only became standard in 1921**, fifty-five years after the scheme began, and the simplified design still in use today was drawn in **1938** by an unnamed student at the Central School of Arts and Crafts. Everything before that was made in whatever the manufacturer of the day could supply.

The scheme's rules are the strictest of any on this page:

* At least **20 years since the subject died**
* A surviving building in **Greater London, outside the City**
* The building must survive **in a form the person would recognise**
* It must be **visible from a public highway**
* **One plaque per person**, and no more than two on any one building

That last rule is modern, and older plaques predate it — **Gandhi has two**, and Palmerston, Gladstone and Thackeray have three each.

## Green: Westminster and the boroughs

Green is the clearest colour signal in London, because one scheme dominates it. Of **296 green plaques**, **114 are the City of Westminster's**, with the **London Borough of Islington** (43) and **Ealing Civic Society** (13) behind it.

Borough schemes matter because their criteria are looser. Where English Heritage commemorates **people only**, and only twenty years after death, a borough will happily plaque a **building, a business or an event** — and does not always require the subject to have died. If you see a plaque for a shop, a studio or a music venue, it is almost certainly a borough or society plaque.

---

## Brown: Hackney now, the Victorians then

Brown splits neatly in two. The modern half is the **London Borough of Hackney's** own scheme (43). The historic half is the original one: **23 from the London County Council** and **15 from the Royal Society of Arts**, the body that started the whole thing in 1866.

The best-known example is **Dr Samuel Johnson at 17 Gough Square** — brown terracotta, put up in **1876**, and the only official-scheme plaque inside the City of London.

## The specialist schemes worth spotting

These are the ones where colour genuinely does tell you something, because a single organisation accounts for nearly all of them:

* **Film cell** — shaped like a strip of celluloid rather than a roundel. **25 of the 29** are the **British Film Institute's**, marking people in film. This is a *shape*, not a colour, which is why it catches people out.
* **Maroon** — **20 of 27** are the **London Borough of Lewisham's**.
* **Marble** — the **Police Memorial Trust**, marking officers killed on duty.
* **Red and black** — the **Institution of Mechanical Engineers**, for engineering history.
* **Pink** — **Highgate Heritage**, and all eight of London's pink plaques are theirs.
* **Red** — no single owner, but the **Transport Trust** is the largest user, marking railway and transport history.
* **Purple** — there is exactly one, from the **Geological Society of London**.
* **Orange** — also exactly one, from the **Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants**.

Two more are worth knowing even though they do not sit neatly on a colour chart. **Rainbow plaques** mark LGBTQIA+ heritage and are unmistakable when you see one. And **private and museum plaques** can look like anything at all — the **221B Baker Street** Sherlock Holmes plaque is the museum's own, and the official scheme will never plaque a fictional character.

> **Historic England's National Blue Plaque Scheme is a different thing again.** It covers England *outside* London, so nothing on this page overlaps with it.

## The test that actually works

Forget the colour. Ask what is being commemorated:

> **If it marks a shop, a band, a building, an event, or somebody still alive, it is not an official English Heritage plaque.** Official plaques mark people, they go up at least twenty years after death, and they say English Heritage on them.

Everything else — and that is well over half of what you will see on a walk across London — belongs to a borough, a trust, a civic society or a private owner. Several are older than the official scheme's current rules, and a few are considerably more interesting.

## About these numbers

The counts here come from **[our own plaques map](/plaques/)**, built on the **Open Plaques** dataset, which is public domain. It covers **3,630 plaques** inside a Greater London bounding box.

Two honest caveats. The dataset is a **2021 snapshot**, so plaques erected since then are not counted — including most of London's **rainbow plaques**, which mark LGBTQIA+ heritage and are a recent addition. And **organisation records are not complete**: several hundred plaques have no issuing body recorded at all, which is why the word "usually" in this guide is doing real work. The pattern is reliable; the precise totals are a floor, not a ceiling.
