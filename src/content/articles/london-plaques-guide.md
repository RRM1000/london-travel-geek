There are around **3,630 commemorative plaques** across Greater London — small round markers noting that someone notable lived, worked or died in a particular building. Most people walk past them without knowing who put them there, why they come in different colours, or what a person has to do to get one.

This guide covers how they work. For the ones worth making a trip for, see **[the plaques worth walking to](/articles/london-blue-plaques/)**.

> 💡 **The Short Version:** English Heritage runs the official scheme, but it is only one of many. Of London's 3,630 plaques, fewer than 1,000 are official ones. The rest come from the City of London Corporation, borough councils and dozens of independent trusts and societies — which is why the colours vary so much.

## Where the scheme started

The official scheme is **the oldest of its kind in the world**, and English Heritage did not start it. **The Society of Arts** did, after the MP William Ewart proposed the idea in Parliament in **1863**. The scheme was founded in **1866** and the first plaques went up in **1867**.

Responsibility passed between bodies as London's government changed: to the **London County Council** in 1903, the **Greater London Council** in 1966, and **English Heritage** in 1986. Plaques from all three eras are still on the walls, which is part of why they do not all look the same.

The early ones were not blue. Makers used brown, terracotta, green, bronze, lead and stone. **Blue ceramic only became standard in 1921**, and the simplified design still used today was drawn in **1938** by an unnamed student at the Central School of Arts and Crafts.

## Who puts them up

| Scheme | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| **English Heritage** | The official scheme. Greater London outside the City. **People only**, dead at least 20 years |
| **City of London Corporation** | The Square Mile, which is excluded from the English Heritage scheme. Commemorates **lost buildings as well as people** |
| **Borough councils** | Much looser criteria — **buildings, businesses and events all qualify**, and the subject has not always died |
| **Nubian Jak Community Trust** | Black and minority-ethnic history. The first was **Bob Marley at County Hall in 2006** |
| **Heritage Foundation** | Entertainers — comedians, actors and TV names |
| **Specialist trusts** | The British Film Institute, the Police Memorial Trust, the Transport Trust, the Institution of Mechanical Engineers and others, each covering their own field |
| **Private and museum plaques** | Anything at all. The **221B Baker Street** Sherlock Holmes plaque is the museum's own |

All of these are genuine. The difference is in the rules each one sets, and those differences are the reason a plaque for a nightclub or a living musician exists at all.

> **Historic England's National Blue Plaque Scheme is a separate thing again.** It covers England *outside* London, so nothing in this guide overlaps with it.

## What the colours mean

Colour generally tells you **which organisation put the plaque up**, not what it commemorates. Some colours belong almost entirely to one body; others are shared.

| Colour | Usually indicates | In London |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Blue** | The official scheme, but also the City of London, Nubian Jak, the Heritage Foundation and several boroughs | 2,045 |
| **Green** | Borough schemes, above all the City of Westminster | 296 |
| **Black** | No single scheme — the Hampstead Plaque Fund and Westminster are the biggest users | 291 |
| **Brown** | Hackney's scheme today; the Victorian originals before 1921 | 175 |
| **Bronze, stone, white, grey** | Mostly one-off and institutional plaques | 384 combined |
| **Film cell** (a strip of celluloid) | The British Film Institute, for people in film | 29 |
| **Maroon** | The London Borough of Lewisham | 27 |
| **Marble** | Often the Police Memorial Trust, for officers killed on duty | 12 |
| **Red and black** | The Institution of Mechanical Engineers | 8 |
| **Pink** | Highgate Heritage — all eight of them | 8 |

### Blue

Blue is much the most common colour, and the official scheme uses it — but several other organisations use it too. Of London's **2,045 blue plaques**, **905** come from the official scheme and the councils that ran it before. The **City of London Corporation** accounts for 158, the **Heritage Foundation** 36 and the **Nubian Jak Community Trust** 34, with borough schemes making up much of the remainder. Camden's are blue and are easily mistaken for official ones.

A blue plaque is therefore worth reading rather than assuming, and the organisation's name is usually written on the plaque itself.

### Green

Green is the clearest signal in London, because one scheme dominates it. Of **296 green plaques**, **114 belong to the City of Westminster**, with the **London Borough of Islington** (43) and **Ealing Civic Society** (13) behind it.

### Brown

Brown splits in two. The modern half is the **London Borough of Hackney's** own scheme (43). The historic half is the original one — **23 from the London County Council** and **15 from the Royal Society of Arts**, the body that began the scheme in 1866. **Dr Samuel Johnson's** plaque at 17 Gough Square is the best-known example, put up in **1876**, and the only official-scheme plaque inside the City of London.

### The official scheme's other colours

**84 official plaques are not blue at all** — 37 brown, 15 black, 9 stone, 7 grey, 7 bronze, 5 white, 3 green and 1 brass. These are the pre-1921 survivors, made before the blue ceramic standard existed.

### The specialist colours

A few colours belong almost entirely to one organisation, which makes them easy to identify:

* **Film cell** — shaped like a strip of celluloid rather than a roundel. **25 of the 29** are the **British Film Institute's**. This is a shape rather than a colour, which is why it catches people out.
* **Maroon** — **20 of 27** are **Lewisham's**.
* **Marble** — the **Police Memorial Trust**.
* **Red and black** — the **Institution of Mechanical Engineers**.
* **Pink** — **Highgate Heritage**, and all eight are theirs.
* **Red** — no single owner, but the **Transport Trust** is the largest user.
* **Purple** and **orange** — one plaque each, from the **Geological Society of London** and the **Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants**.

**Rainbow plaques**, marking LGBTQIA+ heritage, are unmistakable when you see one and are a recent addition to the city.

## How a plaque gets put up

The official scheme has the strictest rules of any on this page. To qualify:

* At least **20 years since the subject died**
* A surviving building in **Greater London, outside the City**
* The building must survive **in a form the person would recognise**
* It must be **visible from a public highway**
* **One plaque per person**, and no more than two on any one building

Churches, schools and theatres are not normally considered, on the grounds that too many people passed through them. **Anyone can nominate someone.**

The two-per-building rule is modern, so older plaques predate it — **Gandhi has two**, and Palmerston, Gladstone and Thackeray have three each.

> **Only 15% of London's official plaques commemorate women.** Since 2016, more than half of new awards have gone to women, which is the scheme correcting a hundred and fifty years of arithmetic.

## Telling them apart on the street

The quickest guide is the subject rather than the colour:

* **A person, twenty or more years dead** — could well be official.
* **A building, a business, a band or an event** — a borough or society plaque. English Heritage does not commemorate these.
* **Somebody still alive** — not official. Several other schemes allow it.
* **A fictional character** — never official. The Sherlock Holmes plaque belongs to the museum.

The organisation's name is normally on the plaque, so the reliable answer is simply to read it.

## About these numbers

The counts here come from the **Open Plaques** dataset, which is public domain, filtered to a Greater London bounding box — the same data behind our map.

Two caveats worth stating. It is a **2021 snapshot**, so plaques erected since then are not counted, rainbow plaques among them. And **organisation records are incomplete**: several hundred plaques have no issuing body recorded, which is why this guide says "usually" and "mostly" rather than giving absolutes. The pattern is reliable; the totals are a floor rather than a ceiling.
