There are **thousands of commemorative plaques** across Greater London — small round markers noting that someone notable lived, worked or died in a particular building. This guide covers who puts them up, what the colours mean and what a person has to do to get one. For the ones worth making a trip for, see **[the plaques worth walking to](/articles/london-blue-plaques/)**.

> 💡 **The Short Version:** English Heritage runs the official scheme, but it is only one of many: about a quarter of London's plaques are official ones. The rest come from the City of London Corporation, borough councils and dozens of independent trusts and societies — which is why the colours vary so much.

## Where the scheme started

The official scheme is thought to be **the oldest of its kind in the world**, and English Heritage did not start it. **The Society of Arts** did, after the MP William Ewart proposed the idea in Parliament in **1863**. The scheme was founded in **1866** and the first plaques went up in **1867**.

Responsibility passed from the Society of Arts to the **London County Council** at the turn of the century, then to the **Greater London Council** in 1966 and **English Heritage** in 1986. Plaques from all four are still on the walls.

The very first plaques were blue, but the colour was expensive and the Society mainly used brown, with terracotta, green, bronze, lead and stone also in use. **Blue ceramic only became standard in 1921**, and the simplified design still used today was drawn in **1938** by a student at the Central School of Arts and Crafts.

## Who puts them up

| Scheme | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| **English Heritage** | The official scheme. Greater London outside the City. **People only**, dead at least 20 years |
| **City of London Corporation** | The Square Mile, which is excluded from the English Heritage scheme. Commemorates **lost buildings as well as people** |
| **Borough councils** | Much looser criteria — **buildings, businesses and events all qualify**, and the subject has not always died |
| **Nubian Jak Community Trust** | Black and minority-ethnic history. The first was **Bob Marley at County Hall in 2006** |
| **Heritage Foundation** | Entertainers — comedians, actors and TV names |
| **Specialist trusts** | The British Film Institute, the Police Memorial Trust, the Transport Trust, the Institution of Mechanical Engineers and others, each covering their own field |
| **Private and museum plaques** | Anything at all. The **221B Baker Street** Sherlock Holmes plaque is the museum's own |

The rules differ, which is why plaques for nightclubs and living musicians exist.

> **Historic England's National Blue Plaque Scheme is separate.** It covers England *outside* London.

## What the colours mean

Colour generally tells you **which organisation put the plaque up**, not what it commemorates. Some colours belong almost entirely to one body; others are shared.

| Colour | In London | Usually indicates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Blue** | 2,045 | The official scheme, but also the City of London, Nubian Jak, the Heritage Foundation and several boroughs |
| **Green** | 296 | Borough schemes, above all the City of Westminster |
| **Black** | 291 | No single scheme — the Hampstead Plaque Fund and Westminster are the biggest users |
| **Brown** | 175 | Hackney's scheme today; the Victorian originals before 1921 |
| **Bronze, stone, white, grey** | 384 combined | Mostly one-off and institutional plaques |
| **Film cell** (a strip of celluloid) | 29 | The British Film Institute, for people in film |
| **Maroon** | 27 | The London Borough of Lewisham |
| **Marble** | 12 | Often the Police Memorial Trust, for officers killed on duty |
| **Red and black** | 8 | The Institution of Mechanical Engineers |
| **Pink** | 8 | Highgate Heritage — all eight of them |

### Blue

Blue is the most common colour, and the official scheme uses it, but several other organisations use it too. Of London's **2,045 blue plaques**, **905** come from the official scheme and the councils that ran it before. The **City of London Corporation** accounts for 158, the **Heritage Foundation** 36 and the **Nubian Jak Community Trust** 34, with borough schemes making up much of the remainder. Camden's are blue and are easily mistaken for official ones. The organisation's name is usually written on the plaque itself.

### Green

One scheme dominates green. Of **296 green plaques**, **114 belong to the City of Westminster**, with the **London Borough of Islington** (43) and **Ealing Civic Society** (13) behind it.

### Brown

Brown is not only Hackney's. The **London Borough of Hackney's** own, modern scheme accounts for 43 of the 175. The old official scheme before 1921 adds **23 from the London County Council** and **15 from the Royal Society of Arts**, the body that began the scheme in 1866 — **Dr Samuel Johnson's** plaque at 17 Gough Square is the best-known example, put up in **1876**, and the only official-scheme plaque inside the City of London. The remaining 94 are split across local trusts, councils and heritage groups, the largest being Harrow Heritage Trust and Transport for London at ten each.

### The official scheme's other colours

**84 official plaques are not blue at all** — 37 brown, 15 black, 9 stone, 7 grey, 7 bronze, 5 white, 3 green and 1 brass. These are the pre-1921 survivors, made before the blue ceramic standard existed.

### The specialist colours

A few colours belong almost entirely to one organisation, which makes them easy to identify:

* **Film cell** — shaped like a strip of celluloid rather than a roundel. **25 of the 29** are the **British Film Institute's**. It is a shape rather than a colour.
* **Maroon** — **20 of 27** are **Lewisham's**.
* **Marble** — the **Police Memorial Trust**.
* **Red and black** — the **Institution of Mechanical Engineers**.
* **Pink** — **Highgate Heritage**, and all eight are theirs.
* **Red** — no single owner, but the **Transport Trust** is the largest user.
* **Purple** and **orange** — one plaque each, from the **Geological Society of London** and the **Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants**.

**Rainbow plaques** mark LGBTQIA+ heritage and are a recent addition to the city.

## How a plaque gets put up

The official scheme has the strictest rules of any on this page. To qualify, a subject needs:

* At least **20 years since death**
* A surviving building in **Greater London, outside the City**
* A building that survives **in a form the person would recognise**
* A building **visible from a public highway**
* **One plaque per person**, and no more than two on any one building

Churches, schools and theatres are not normally considered. **Anyone can nominate someone.**

The two-per-building rule is modern, so older plaques predate it — **Gandhi has two**, and Palmerston, Gladstone and Thackeray have three each.

> **Only 15% of London's official plaques commemorate women.** Since 2016, more than half of new awards have gone to women.

## Telling them apart on the street

The quickest guide is the subject rather than the colour:

* **A person, twenty or more years dead** — could well be official.
* **A building, a business, a band or an event** — a borough or society plaque. English Heritage does not commemorate these.
* **Somebody still alive** — not official. Several other schemes allow it.
* **A fictional character** — never official. The Sherlock Holmes plaque belongs to the museum.

The reliable check is the organisation's name on the plaque.

## Seeing them

Plaques are on public streets and free to look at.

Nearly all are on private homes, but some are museums you can go into, including **Handel Hendrix House** in Mayfair, the **Charles Dickens Museum** in Bloomsbury, **Keats House** in Hampstead and the **Freud Museum**.
